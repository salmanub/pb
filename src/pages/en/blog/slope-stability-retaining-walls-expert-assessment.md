## When does a slope stop being ground and become a problem?

A slope is any terrain surface inclined with respect to the horizontal. We see them every day—in terraced gardens, plots with grade changes, embankments, roads—and we rarely give them a second thought. **Until something changes.**

A neighbour digs at the base. A swimming pool leaks for months without anyone noticing. A decades-old party wall starts to lean. Cracks that weren't there before appear in the garden floor.

When a slope's equilibrium breaks, **the consequences can be catastrophic**—and the window for action is narrow.

## A real case: pool at the crest, excavation at the toe

A few days ago we attended a case that perfectly illustrates how several factors combine to create a serious risk situation:

**The situation:**
- A house on an elevated plot, with a **party wall** bridging a drop of approximately **6 metres** to the neighbour below.
- At the top of the slope, our client has a **swimming pool** that has been **leaking water** for months through micro-cracks in the shell.
- The lower neighbour has **excavated a vegetable garden at the toe of the wall**, removing supporting material without any prior study.

**The symptoms:**
- New cracks in the party wall.
- The crest terrain shows **settlement** (small depressions next to the pool).
- **Persistent moisture** appears on the lower face of the wall facing the neighbour's garden.

**The diagnosis:** We are dealing with a **slope affected by three simultaneous destabilising factors**:

1. **Surcharge at the crest** — The weight of the pool (over 40 tonnes when filled) generates additional thrust on the wall.
2. **Soil saturation** — Water loss from the pool saturates the ground, reduces cohesion and increases hydrostatic pressure against the back of the wall.
3. **Toe removal** — The garden excavation has eliminated the soil wedge that acted as a natural counterweight, drastically reducing the factor of safety.

**If no action is taken, the wall may collapse.** This is not an exaggeration—it is basic soil mechanics.

## What destabilises a slope? The most common causes

### External causes (human or environmental action)

| Cause | Failure mechanism | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| **Excavation at the toe** | Removes passive support wedge. Factor of safety drops sharply. | Very high |
| **Surcharge at the crest** | Pools, buildings, stockpiled materials, heavy vehicles. | High |
| **Water infiltration** | Pools, broken pipes, excessive irrigation. Saturates the ground. | High |
| **Prolonged torrential rain** | Massive infiltration. Raises the water table and generates pore pressure. | Medium-high |
| **Vibrations** | Nearby construction, heavy traffic, blasting. | Medium |
| **Vegetation removal** | Roots bind the superficial soil. Without them, accelerated erosion. | Medium |

### Internal causes (soil properties)

- **Expansive clay soils** — Swell with water and lose strength when saturated.
- **Uncontrolled fills** — Common in developments: soil dumped without compaction or drainage.
- **Alternating permeable/impermeable layers** — Water flows through permeable layers and creates slip planes over impermeable ones.
- **Weathering** — Over time, intact rock degrades and loses bearing capacity.

## Stability analysis: what we do and how

### Phase 1 — Site visit and in-situ inspection

A slope cannot be analysed from an office. The **site visit** is the essential first step:

- **Slope geometry**: height, angle of inclination, presence of berms or steps, distance to structures.
- **Condition of retaining wall** (if present): cracks, tilting, bulging, open joints, moisture stains, efflorescence.
- **Visible drainage**: are there weep holes? Do they work? Are water seepages visible?
- **Immediate surroundings**: recent excavations, surcharges, pools, buried services, vegetation.
- **Signs of movement**: ground cracks (arcuate), tilting trees, ground steps, deformation of rigid elements (pavements, kerbs, posts).
- **Comprehensive photographic documentation** with georeferencing.

### Phase 2 — Study and analysis

With the field data, we carry out:

1. **Geotechnical characterisation** — From existing studies or, if unavailable, trial pits and field testing (dynamic penetrometer, SPT test).

2. **Stability calculation model** — Factor of safety analysis against circular, planar or wedge failure using limit equilibrium methods (Bishop, Janbu, Morgenstern-Price).

3. **Thrust analysis on walls** — Active and passive earth pressure calculations (Rankine, Coulomb), considering water table, crest surcharges and toe excavation effects.

4. **Structural verification of the wall** — Can it resist current thrusts? What if the water level rises?

5. **Risk scenarios** — We model the current situation and several future scenarios.

### Phase 3 — Expert report and proposed action

We deliver a **complete expert report** including:

- **Diagnosis** with the root cause, documented with evidence.
- **Stability calculations** for slope and wall, with plans and sections.
- **Risk classification**: immediate, short-term or medium-term.
- **Proposed action** with detailed engineering: stabilisation, drainage, reinforcement or underpinning solutions, with cost estimates.
- **Liability assessment** — Essential if the problem results from a neighbour's, builder's or third party's actions.

> The report has full expert validity and can be submitted as evidence in judicial or extrajudicial proceedings.

## Stabilisation solutions

### Drainage (the most effective measure)

- **Horizontal drains** — Perforated tubes driven into the slope to capture and evacuate groundwater.
- **Drainage trenches** — At the toe or base of the wall, with drain pipe and granular fill.
- **Weep holes** — Tubes through the wall to relieve hydrostatic pressure behind it.
- **Crest ditches** — Channel surface water to prevent infiltration at the slope head.

### Retention and reinforcement

- **Riprap or gabion walls** — Cost-effective for slopes up to 4-5 m.
- **Reinforced concrete walls** — For significant grade changes.
- **Reinforced earth / mechanically stabilised earth** — Geotextiles + compacted fill layers.
- **Rock bolts and anchors** — Steel bars anchored into competent rock or soil.
- **Micropiles** — To underpin existing wall foundations.

### Re-profiling and geometry

- **Slope flattening** — Reduce the gradient.
- **Equilibrium berm** — Intermediate horizontal platform.
- **Toe fill** — Add heavy compacted material as counterweight.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a slope stability study cost?

It depends on the complexity: a straightforward analysis with site visit + report starts from **€800–1,200**. If field geotechnical investigation is needed (trial pits, testing), costs rise to **€2,000–4,000**. What always costs more is failing to act in time.

### Can I claim against a neighbour who excavated at the toe of my wall?

Yes. If the excavation has altered the slope equilibrium and causes damage to your property, **Article 1902 of the Spanish Civil Code** establishes the obligation to repair the damage caused. You will need an expert report proving the causal link.

### Can a swimming pool destabilise a slope?

Absolutely. An 8×4 m pool with 1.5 m depth holds 48,000 litres = 48 tonnes of concentrated weight. If it also leaks, **it saturates the soil, reduces cohesion and multiplies hydrostatic pressure** on the wall. A very dangerous combination.
